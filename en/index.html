Abstain-R1: Calibrated Abstention and Post-Refusal Clarification via Verifiable RL

Problem: models either answer anyway or stop with “cannot answer”; what is missing is calibratable behaviour that abstains when appropriate and then asks for the missing information.
Where the evidence sits: the abstract reports a 3B model trained with a clarification-aware RLVR reward that improves abstention and clarification on unanswerable queries while preserving strong performance on answerable ones; it is verifiable-reward RL, not a prompt template.
What the authors say about limits: 3B scale, not directly extrapolable to 70B+; training and evaluation centre on unanswerable queries, with limited coverage of open-domain chat and multi-turn clarification; the reward depends on verifiable answer/clarification labels, which carry noise; no production deployment is reported.
What it does not claim: a general assistant model; a solution to all hallucinations; that more abstention is always better; an auto-alignment method that needs no human evaluation.

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback

Problem: training a harmless assistant normally needs tens of thousands of human harmlessness labels; can a small set of natural language principles be enough, with the model critiquing and revising its own responses and a preference model trained on AI-generated labels driving the RL stage.
Where the evidence sits: the pipeline has two stages — the supervised stage (self-critique and revision, SL-CAI) in Section 3 and the RLAIF stage in Section 4; the main result is the harmlessness-versus-helpfulness Elo scatter in Figure 2 (all 52B RL runs, with the caption stating that only differences are meaningful), whose caption reads "The RL-CAI models trained with AI feedback learn to be less harmful at a given level of helpfulness."; Elo comparisons and the chain-of-thought tradeoff are in 4.3, evasiveness and the change of labeling instructions in 4.4, and absolute harmfulness scores in 4.5; discussion and future directions are in Section 6 and 6.1, and dual-use risk in 6.2. There is no separate Limitations section; Section 6 onward is the discussion of limits and future directions.
What the authors say about limits: Section 6 states that although human feedback labels for harmlessness were removed, this work still relied on human supervision in the form of helpfulness labels, and achieving helpfulness and instruction-following without human feedback is left to future work; footnote 2 in 1.1 acknowledges the principles were chosen in a fairly ad hoc and iterative way for research purposes and should be redeveloped by a larger set of stakeholders and adapted to the intended usage and deployment location; 4.3 reports that RL-CAI models can be over-trained, producing Goodharting behaviour such as overly harsh responses to harmful prompts or boilerplate language after most red-team prompts; 4.5 cautions that absolute harmfulness scores may not be well calibrated because different workers have personal biases about the 0-4 scale; 6.1 lists robustness — whether models can be made essentially immune to red-team attacks — as a remaining issue; and 6.2 notes that reducing the need for human feedback also makes it easier to deploy systems that have not been thoroughly tested and observed by humans, and that the methods are dual use.
What it does not claim: the paper does not claim alignment without any human supervision — helpfulness labels still come from humans, and the authors write that the goal is not to remove human supervision entirely but to make it more efficient, transparent and targeted; it does not claim CAI models are better than RLHF on every axis, and 4.3 says RL-CAI with CoT seems slightly less helpful but slightly more harmless than without CoT; Elo is a relative score whose caption says only differences are meaningful, and 4.4 explains that the labeling instructions were changed (prefer the less evasive response when both are equally harmless) and the vendor changed, so these Elo scores are not directly comparable with prior work; and immunity to red-team attacks is a future direction, not a result of this paper.

Proving and Improving the Platonic Representation Hypothesis with JEPAs

Problem: are models across modalities converging on a shared “Platonic” representation space? If so, can a small model catch up with a large one by using better training views?
Where the evidence sits: the abstract reports a 109M-parameter text JEPA that aligns with DINOv2 and CLIP image encoders better than every language model the authors evaluated, up to 72B; the same abstract states that good views need 42 times fewer training samples than next-token prediction. The body uses JEPA as a provable/improveable instance rather than a correlation-only observation.
What the authors say about limits: it is a preprint; the text JEPA is 109M, far smaller than the LLMs it is compared with; the alignment metric measures representation similarity, not generation quality; 42× is sample efficiency under specific views and data, not a universal constant; it does not prove every modality converges to one space.
What it does not claim: a new state-of-the-art language model; a replacement for next-token prediction; that small models beat large models across the board; a final proof of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis.

Bernini: Latent Semantic Planning for Video Diffusion

Problem: multimodal LLMs reason over semantics and diffusion models render pixels, but the two families remain separate; video generation and editing lack a division of labour between semantic planning and rendering.
Where the evidence sits: Bernini’s MLLM planner predicts the target semantic representation directly in ViT embedding space, and a DiT renderer synthesises pixels conditioned on that plan plus text features and, for editing, source VAE features; semantics act as the interface so planner and renderer train separately with light co-training; SA-3D RoPE handles multiple visual inputs and CoT is added to the planner; the paper reports state of the art on several generation and editing benchmarks.
What the authors say about limits: both planner and renderer are large, so training and inference are costly; an interpretable semantic interface is not the same as controllable output; SOTA depends on the chosen benchmarks; editing relies heavily on source VAE features; no long-video or real-time reporting.
What it does not claim: a general video editor; that semantic planning always beats pixel-space diffusion; that it is open-source or commercially usable without checking the licence.

Scaling laws

Language Models are Few-Shot Learners

Problem: after scaling a model up, can tasks be performed close to the level of task-specific fine-tuning with no gradient updates and only a few examples placed in the input.
Where the evidence sits: the abstract gives the 175B scale and the no-gradient-update, no-fine-tuning setting; results across nine task categories are in Section 3 (LAMBADA in Figure 3.2 and Table 3.2, open-domain QA in Table 3.3, SuperGLUE in Table 3.8, reading comprehension in Table 3.7, arithmetic in 3.9.1 and Table 3.9); train-test overlap analysis is in Section 4 and Figure 4.2; the limits have a dedicated Section 5 Limitations, and societal impact is in Section 6.
What the authors say about limits: Section 5 lists several layers — long-form synthesis still repeats itself semantically, loses coherence over sufficiently long passages and contradicts itself; on comparison tasks such as WIC and ANLI and on a subset of reading comprehension tasks, one-shot and even few-shot performance does little better than chance; only autoregressive models were studied, so the experiments include no bidirectional architectures or denoising objectives, which the authors acknowledge may cost performance on tasks that benefit from bidirectionality, and they conjecture a large bidirectional model would fine-tune better; the pretraining objective weights every token equally and may hit the limits of the objective itself, and the model is not grounded in video or physical interaction; pretraining sample efficiency remains far below a human's; models at this scale are expensive and inconvenient to run inference on; and decisions are not easily interpretable, not necessarily well calibrated on novel inputs, and retain the biases of the training data. Section 5 also states an explicit uncertainty: whether few-shot learning actually learns new tasks from scratch at inference time or simply recognises tasks learned during training is unclear and may vary by task. Section 4 explains that decontamination was only partial — a bug meant only part of the detected overlap was removed, retraining was not feasible given the cost, so clean subsets were built afterwards using 13-gram overlap and re-evaluated, with PIQA and Winograd flagged as possibly contaminated and asterisked in Section 3.
What it does not claim: the abstract says sometimes even reaching competitiveness with prior fine-tuning approaches, not beating fine-tuning on every task; the paper does not show that few-shot prompting equals learning a new task at inference time, and Section 5 leaves that as an unresolved ambiguity; it does not claim training and test data were fully separated; the experiments cover autoregressive language models only and cannot be extended to bidirectional architectures or other training objectives; and Section 3 also reports tasks where few-shot learning still struggles, including ANLI, RACE and QuAC.

ChainDoRA: Tensor-Train Factorized Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation for Parameter-Efficient LLM Fine-Tuning

Problem: LoRA uses two low-rank matrices; DoRA separates weight magnitude and direction, but its directional branch keeps a dense LoRA-style factorization, so the parameter cost does not really come down.
Where the evidence sits: the abstract reports a controlled setting (15,119 response-only examples, LLaMA-7B, seven commonsense-reasoning benchmarks): TT rank 16 reaches a seven-task average of 72.30% versus 69.88% for LoRA and 69.39% for DoRA, with 5.35M trainable parameters versus 56.10M and 56.98M, a 90.62% reduction against DoRA.
What the authors say about limits: one 7B model and one 15,119-example adaptation set; the tasks are commonsense reasoning, with no instruction following, multilingual or long-context coverage; ablations only vary TT rank and adapter placement; no production deployment is reported.
What it does not claim: state of the art for every PEFT setting; a wholesale replacement for DoRA; a full training-throughput or memory evaluation; a result that extrapolates across model scales.

ContextWeaver: Selective and Dependency-Structured Memory Construction for LLM Agents

Problem: coding agents accumulate long context, and sliding windows or prompt compression drop earlier information that later steps depend on; ordinary retrieval sees similarity but not causal dependencies between reasoning steps.
Where the evidence sits: ContextWeaver turns an interaction trace into a dependency graph of reasoning steps, links each step to earlier steps it relies on, builds compact dependency summaries and adds a lightweight validation layer using execution feedback; it beats a sliding-window baseline on all five software-engineering and terminal benchmarks, uses 13–18% fewer agent steps and raises SWE-Bench Pro resolve rate from 66.5% to 72.6% across three model backbones.
What the authors say about limits: evaluation covers five SWE/terminal benchmarks under a fixed task budget; graph construction depends on step segmentation and tool feedback; open-ended research, multi-user collaboration and production environments are not covered; it is not end-to-end trained.
What it does not claim: not that every context should be graph-structured; not that 72.6% represents every repository task; not that fewer steps necessarily means proportionally lower cost.

FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness

Problem: self-attention time and memory grow quadratically with sequence length, and the bottleneck is memory traffic rather than arithmetic; can exact attention be computed without writing the N×N matrix back to HBM.
Where the evidence sits: the abstract gives three end-to-end speed-ups plus a 7.6× speed-up on the attention computation itself; the limits are in Section 5, Limitations and Future Directions.
What the authors say about limits: three — each new attention implementation needs a new CUDA kernel written in a much lower-level language than PyTorch, which is significant engineering effort; implementations may not transfer across GPU architectures; and their IO-aware version is optimal within constants on a single GPU, whereas the attention computation itself may be parallelisable across multiple GPUs.
What it does not claim: the paper does not present an approximation — the result is exact — but it also does not solve multi-GPU partitioning, and it does not equate end-to-end speed-up with kernel speed-up (the 15% / 3× / 2.4× figures and the 7.6× kernel figure are different measurements and must not be mixed).

Data Darwinism Part II: DataEvolve — AI can Autonomously Evolve Pretraining Data Curation

Problem: Data Darwinism Part I gave a ten-level processing ladder but still required manual strategy design for a single category; modern corpora have hundreds of heterogeneous categories, making manual design prohibitive.
Where the evidence sits: a closed evolutionary loop identifies quality issues, generates candidate strategies, executes on sampled data, evaluates and refines across generations, accumulating an issue pool and a strategy pool; on 8 Nemotron-CC categories and 672B tokens it runs 30 iterations per category to produce the 504B-token Darwin-CC; 3B models trained on 500B tokens average 44.13 across 18 benchmarks, beating DCLM, Ultra-FineWeb and FineWeb-Edu and gaining 3.96 over raw data.

What it does not claim: that AI generates data; that any corpus can use the same loop; that 44.13 is comparable across benchmark suites.

DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report

Problem: once an MoE language model is scaled up, how do you control training cost, inference cost and load balance instead of only adding parameters.

What the authors say about limits: Section 6 states that deliberate search "might not be necessary for many existing tasks that GPT-4 already excels at" and that, as an initial step, only three relatively simple tasks are explored; Appendix B.3 states ToT "could require 5-100 times more generated tokens than CoT"; the work uses an off-the-shelf LM, with fine-tuning left as a future direction.
What it does not claim: 74% is the success rate on the 100-game hard subset with BFS keeping b=5 candidates per step, not a result on all 1,362 games; the paper does not show ToT beats CoT everywhere — Appendix B.1 reports only slight gains on GSM8K/StrategyQA and Appendix B.2 reports GPT-3.5+ToT at 19% on Game of 24; the authors note general crosswords can be solved by specialized NLP pipelines, and no training-level claim is made.

YaRN: Efficient Context Window Extension of Large Language Models

Problem: RoPE models fail beyond their training length; how to extend the context window with fewer tokens and training steps?
Where the evidence sits: the abstract claims 10x fewer tokens and 2.5x fewer training steps than prior methods; LLaMA models extrapolate far beyond their original pretraining length; it surpasses prior SOTA on context extension and can extrapolate beyond the fine-tuning dataset context.
What the authors say about limits: mainly LLaMA-family experiments; requires light fine-tuning; long contexts still pay attention cost; fidelity on all tasks is not proven.
What it does not claim: not zero-cost infinite context; not fine-tuning-free; not universal across models.
